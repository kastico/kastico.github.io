There's something I've noticed about the best work I've done: it always starts with a constraint.

Not the kind imposed by deadlines or budgets, but the kind you choose yourself. A deliberate reduction of possibility that forces you to think harder about what matters.

## The Tyranny of Options

When you have every option available, it's easy to spend all your energy choosing rather than building. I spent months tweaking colour palettes for projects that never shipped because I couldn't decide between forty shades of blue.

The paradox is that **fewer choices produces better work**. Not because limitation makes you settle, but because it makes you commit.

---

I learned this the hard way. My first serious project had no constraints at all. Infinite scope, infinite stack, infinite time. It became infinite in another way too: infinitely unfinished.

The second project I gave myself three rules:
1. Ship in two weeks
2. No dependencies
3. One colour

It shipped. It wasn't perfect. But it existed, and that existence made everything after it easier.

## On Starting

The hardest part is not the middle. It's the beginning, before you've committed to anything. Once you've written the first function, the first sentence, the first line, the rest follows more naturally. Momentum is real.

Start badly. Start wrong. Just start.

The edit is always easier than the blank page.

---

I still catch myself overthinking. It's an occupational hazard. But I've built enough things now to know that the version of the project in my head will always be better than any version I could actually build — and that the only way to close that gap is to build more, faster, and with less self-consciousness.

Build with intent. Ship with humility. Repeat.
